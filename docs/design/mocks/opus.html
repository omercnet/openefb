כנףwing. The leading edge is the part that meets the air first.

ThesisKanaf shows the next ten minutes, not the last second. The panel already shows altitude, speed and heading. Nothing shows what the airplane is about to meet, and when. So every screen is laid out along one axis, the flight line: ruled in minutes in flight, unrolled flat like a 1930s strip map on the ground.

Thrown awayThe instrument strip as hero, the tab bar (now three tenses: Next, Now, Flown), floating round buttons, bottom sheets, list-and-detail screens, the map as a backdrop.

Riskiest assumptionA prediction from noisy GPS vertical speed and groundspeed in bumpy air stays steady enough that a pilot trusts "Class B floor in 1:50" instead of a countdown that jumps. Hence every prediction states its assumption on screen.

One axisAhead is up in flight, ahead is along the line on the ground. In Hebrew the unrolled line starts on the right.
Future is loudBig type is reserved for time-to-something. Present-tense numbers are small, because the panel already shows them.
Assumptions shownEvery prediction says what it assumes. Stale input turns the prediction off, never shows it as current.
Colour is meaningAmber = caution, red = warning, magenta = route, chart blue = airspace. Everything else is ink on paper.
01

Now · in flight

iPad landscape, day. Near KPAO, 2,450 ft, 98 kt, track 312°, +300 fpm, KOAK 18.4 NM.
The idea

The map is track-up and ruled in minutes along your track. The column to its right is the same ruler turned sideways: time goes up, altitude goes across, and airspace is drawn as walls at their floors. The minute marks line up across the edge, so the map and the corridor are one picture at the same scale.

What you read in a glance

The amber block: you meet the SFO Class B floor in 1:50 if nothing changes, and what to do about it. Then the corridor: the floor drops to 1,500 at 6:12, so leveling at 2,900 only delays the problem. The present (GS, TRK, ALT) sits at the trailing edge, small, because the panel already shows it.

Replaces

The bottom instrument strip, the floating 60pt circles (now square tabs fixed to the left edge, still 64pt), the NRST box (now one slab with the field, distance and bearing), and the map as a backdrop under chrome. Zooming the map rescales the corridor too.

02

Now · night

Same moment, night palette.
The idea

Night is not the day screen inverted. Nothing on the screen is brighter than dim warm grey, the chart is near black, and the airspace and route keep their hue at a third of the luminance.

Caution without glare

A filled amber block at night would be the brightest thing in the cockpit and cost dark adaptation. At night caution becomes an amber rule and amber type on black: same meaning, same position, about a tenth of the light.

Replaces

The system dark scheme with navy glass controls and a dimmed raster. Here the palette is one set of tokens, so every element has a measured night value instead of whatever the system dark mode produces.

03

Flown · CVFR debrief

iPad landscape, day. LLHZ to LLIB via TAVOR and DESHE.
The idea

After landing the line is unrolled. One horizontal axis carries three bands: the strip map (where you were across the route), the profile (where you were in height, against the assigned altitude and the ground), and the radio (what you said at each point). Every finding sits at the place it happened.

Honest about the bad leg

There is no overall score that could hide a 71% leg. The verdict names the two things to look at; the low stretch on Tavor to Deshe is drawn as an amber area with its depth and duration, and DESHE says "not heard" only because a transcript exists. Aerodrome zones are hatched and labelled "not scored".

Replaces

The list-and-detail flight screen and the debrief table of rows. Leg 1 is drawn at half scale with a marked break so the short legs where things happened get the room. Replay starts from the point you tap.

04

Now · iPhone

Portrait, day. Same moment as 01.
The idea

No room for a corridor beside the map, so it folds twice. The minutes go onto the track line itself, with the walls marked where you will meet them. The altitude axis becomes one horizontal bar inside the amber block: your altitude, your climb and the floor, read left to right like a tape.

Same order of reading

Top to bottom is future to present: the next thing you will meet, then the map ruled in minutes, then the present at the bottom edge, then NRST at the thumb. The only thing over the map is the destination tag beside its airport.

Replaces

The compact layout that squeezed the iPad strip and NRST box into a phone. Layout still switches on size class, not device: an iPad in Split View gets this screen.

Hard parts, honestly

MapLibre draws the chart, rings, route and track line; the corridor and the amber block are SwiftUI Canvas. The minute marks only line up if both read the same camera scale every frame, which means driving the corridor from MLNMapView camera changes and never animating one without the other.
