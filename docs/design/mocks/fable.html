The log is the spine כנף · Kanaf

  1. One idea. A VFR flight is a nav log: a column of waypoints with a leg between each pair. Kanaf shows that one object in three tenses, planned, flying, flown, and nothing else. The strip is pinned to the chart's edge like a flight-progress strip in a bay; the active leg's cell is where the readouts live, because the only question at a glance is "where am I on this leg, how high, how fast, pointed where".
  2. The home screen is the log itself: strips stacked, tomorrow's plan on top, today's flight live, last month's flights below with their grades. Tap a strip and it becomes the spine; the chart takes the rest of the screen. An airport is a row's endpoint opened up. A debrief is the same strip with the actuals written in. A logbook is the stack with the totals footer.
  3. Thrown away deliberately: the tab bar, the floating round buttons, the bottom instrument strip, bottom sheets, list-and-detail screens, the layer panel, the separate NRST box. The map is no longer a backdrop with chrome floating on it; it is one half of a two-part instrument, chart and log, the way a kneeboard sits beside a sectional.
  4. Kept, because it is safety: red only for warnings, amber only for cautions, stale never shown as current (the footer says what the GPS is doing right now), 60pt targets in flight, 4.5:1 text, a night palette that dims the chart and keeps the paper near-black, Dynamic Type through the strip's rows, mirrored to the right edge for Hebrew.
  5. The wing (כנף) is the ownship glyph on the chart and the marker that slides down the leg's gauge in the strip: the same shape in both places, so the eye can match them in one saccade.
  6. Riskiest assumption: that a 300pt strip permanently owning the chart's edge beats a full-width map with a bottom bar. It costs 25% of the width on a 13" iPad and about 18% on an iPhone, and readouts inside a log cell must read as fast as a dedicated instrument strip. If a pilot in a yoke mount wants the chart edge-to-edge, this idea is wrong.
  7. Type and colour: Big Shoulders Display for identifiers and readouts (tall, condensed, built for signage), Archivo for labels, Azeret Mono for times and log data, Rubik for Hebrew. Day chrome is cool paper, not cream; the route stays magenta because that is what a course line is on a sectional.

The idea

The strip on the left is the flight: KPAO flown, KOAK active, KSTS ahead. The active cell holds the four numbers a pilot glances at, at 56 to 72pt, with the leg's gauge beside them; the wing marker on the gauge is the same glyph as ownship on the chart. The chart is track-up around ownship with a compass ring drawn on the map itself, not a widget.

Nothing floats over the chart. Pan or pinch anywhere; the strip never moves. Tap a row to open that airport in place of the strip (same width, same column); the chart stays live beside it.

What it replaces

The bottom instrument strip, the six floating round buttons, the NRST box, the layer panel and the tab bar. Layers and night mode are two 60pt cells in the strip's footer, next to the GPS state, which is the first thing the footer says: when the fix goes stale the whole active cell greys and the footer reads GPS LOST 14 S in red.

Direct-to is "insert a row": tap NRST, the airport becomes the next waypoint above KOAK, and the gauge restarts.

D · In flight, iPad, night

Same screen, night palette. Chart dimmed at the raster, paper near-black, text warm grey; red and amber keep their meaning and their contrast.

The idea

Night is not a theme swap of white to black panels. The chart is dimmed to 42% brightness and desaturated, the strip's paper goes to #0F1215 with #CFCBC2 ink (13.6:1), the active cell is a shade lighter so it still reads as "this one". The wing on the gauge and the course line stay magenta so the eye can find the two moving things.

What it replaces

The separate night toggle button. Night lives in the footer cell as a state (AUTO, from sun elevation) that can be tapped to override; it is a fact about the display, shown next to the other facts.

The idea

The debrief is not a report page. It is the flight's own strip with the actuals written in the right-hand column where the planned values were, and a grade per leg at the size of a readout. The profile on the right is the same legs laid on their side: planned step line, flown trace, the ±200 ft band, the four low minutes in amber. The AI paragraph sits under the facts it is allowed to use.

Hebrew names ride beside the idents in the strip; in a Hebrew UI the whole strip mirrors to the right edge and the bay reads top to bottom as before.

What it replaces

Flights → History → detail → debrief tab, and the 100%-over-a-0%-leg summary. "Not heard" is amber with a triangle and is distinguished from "not checked" (no transcript) in the text, never red. Replay starts from a waypoint, because that is how a pilot remembers the flight, not from 00:00.

The idea

The same object, folded for a phone in a lap: the rail keeps the flight's order visible with the gauge in the active row, the cell at the bottom keeps the four numbers at 46pt where a thumb can also hit NRST (60pt). The chart keeps the top 70% of the screen with the compass ring and the next waypoint in view.

What it replaces

The iPhone instrument strip plus HUD plus floating buttons, which together took three stacked bands. Here there are two surfaces and the chart.

Also implied, not drawn

The home screen: a column of strips (planned on top, past below) in this same row grammar, which doubles as the logbook with a totals footer. The airport: the strip's column replaced by the A/FD-style block for that row (weather with its age on the first line, frequencies as a label column, runways as a diagram), the chart still live beside it. Plan: the strip with empty actual columns, and the FPL text as a secondary action under "Activate".
